A neutral, practitioner-first map of the Salesforce platform: what they bought, what it is called now, how the pieces fit, and what is actually worth using.

Salesforce started as a single CRM in 1999. Since roughly 2010 it has bought its way into nearly every adjacent category, marketing, commerce, integration, analytics, collaboration, data, and now AI agents, to become a full customer platform rather than a system of record. Most of the products you hear named are acquisitions that were renamed and folded toward the core.

That scale is exactly why it is hard to navigate. The same product has been renamed five or six times. A capability you need might be native and excellent, or a recent bolt-on that is still being woven in, or a long promise that has historically been hard to deliver. This tool keeps a consistent, honest signal on every product so a sales or marketing leader can decide what to lean on and what to scrutinize.

At Dreamforce in October 2025, Salesforce did something bigger than another product launch. It started retiring the word "Cloud." Sales Cloud became Agentforce Sales, Service Cloud became Agentforce Service, Data Cloud became Data 360, and the whole thing was wrapped as Agentforce 360. Marc Benioff has openly mused that the company itself might one day be called Agentforce. The pitch is a move from software you click through to autonomous agents that act on your behalf.

For an architect, the practical takeaway is this. Salesforce now wants to be the orchestration and agent layer over your data, and it is willing to sit on top of a warehouse it does not own through zero-copy. That changes the build-versus-buy math, especially if your real data foundation already lives in Snowflake or Databricks.

What "headless" actually means here

Headless means separating the content and logic from where it is rendered, so the same approved building blocks can be assembled into a web page, an email, a mobile screen, or an in-app message on demand. Salesforce branded this Headless 360 and is buying Contentful, a composable content platform, to supply the building blocks. The story they tell: Data 360 provides the context, Agentforce decides what to say, and Contentful holds the sanctioned content the agent assembles into a one-to-one experience.

The architect's reality check

This vision spans several pending or newly acquired pieces (Contentful, Fin, m3ter, parts of Informatica) plus a rebrand that is still settling. The promise is coherent. The integration is not yet proven at the depth the keynote implies. If your data foundation already lives in a warehouse, the most durable pattern is to let that stay the source of truth and treat Data 360 plus Agentforce as an activation and orchestration layer via zero-copy, rather than re-platforming your data into Salesforce.

This section is here because the average enterprise leader underrates one thing about a platform this large: the attack surface grows with every app you connect to it. Across 2025 and 2026, the pattern in the headlines is consistent, and it is worth understanding clearly.

In most of these incidents, Salesforce's core platform was not breached directly. The two real exposures were over-permissive configuration (public Experience Cloud sites left too open) and the third-party connected-app attack surface. Attackers stole OAuth tokens from trusted integrations and used that borrowed trust to pull data through the API. For a sales or marketing tech leader, that reframes the buying question: every tool you bolt on is another set of keys to your CRM.

The third-party integration playbook, once

An attacker compromises a trusted SaaS app that holds OAuth tokens into many customers' Salesforce orgs. Using those tokens, they query the API and export records, often combing support-case text for embedded secrets like cloud keys and warehouse tokens. It looks like a normal integration doing normal work, so it evades typical defenses. Security researchers put the blast radius of this pattern at roughly ten times a direct attack, because one compromised vendor reaches hundreds of downstream orgs.

The three integration breaches to know

The 2026 direct campaign: Experience Cloud guest users

Separately from the OAuth thefts, 2026 saw a wave of data theft that abused customers' own Experience Cloud guest-user settings on public-facing sites. The extortion group ShinyHunters has claimed roughly a hundred high-profile targets. Many victims disclose it only as a "third-party CRM" incident. A condensed timeline of what has been reported.

What to actually do about it

Harden the configuration

Audit guest-user profiles down to the minimum objects and fields. Set org-wide defaults to Private for external users. Disable the guest profile's API access. Turn off portal and site user visibility. Disable self-registration if your site does not truly need it.

Govern the integrations

Inventory every connected app with OAuth access. Scope each token to the least it needs. Rotate and revoke aggressively, especially dormant and legacy credentials. Monitor API usage for unusual bulk exports. Treat support-case text as sensitive, because attackers mine it for secrets.
